01History of AI

1943 – 1956

Birth

  • 1943McCulloch & Pitts: first model of a neuron (threshold).
  • 1950Turing test is proposed.
  • 1956The term artificial intelligence is coined.
1956 – 1974

Golden years

  • 1958Rosenblatt's perceptron — the weights are learned.
  • 1965First expert system (if–else rules).
  • 1969Minsky & Papert, Perceptrons: a single-layer perceptron can only separate data that is linearly separable, so it cannot compute XOR.
1974 – 1980

First AI winter

  • Combinatorial explosion: rule-based methods scale exponentially.
  • Hardware had to catch up to the software.
1980 – 1993

Expert systems: boom and bust

  • 1980–87The era of expert systems.
  • 1986Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams popularize backpropagation. Multi-layer networks become trainable — 17 years after the XOR result.
  • 1987–93Expert systems prove too expensive to maintain: every case needs separate handling.
1990 – 1999

Statistical turn

  • Early 90sChuck the idea of general intelligence in favour of narrow problems.
  • 1995Support vector machines (Cortes & Vapnik).
2000 – 2011

Data era

  • 2007CUDA: GPUs become programmable.
  • 2009ImageNet: 14 million labelled images.
2012 – 2019

Deep learning revolution

  • 2012AlexNet: error rate falls from ~26% to ~16%.
  • 2017Attention Is All You Need — the Transformer.
  • 2018BERT and GPT-1: pre-train on unlabelled text, fine-tune downstream.
2020 – 2024

Scale

  • 2020GPT-3.
  • 2022ChatGPT — RLHF plus a chat interface.
  • 2024Reasoning models.
2025 – now

Agents

  • 2025Shift from question-answering models to models that act: multi-step tasks.
  • 2026On-device inference.

02Overview of ML

Artificial intelligence

Any method that tries to replicate the results of some aspect of human cognition.

Russell & Norvig

The study of agents that receive percepts from the environment and perform actions.

Elaine Rich

The study of how to make computers do things at which, at the moment, people are better.

Machine learning

Tom Mitchell

A computer program is said to learn from experience E with respect to some class of tasks T and performance measure P, if its performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E.

Kevin Murphy

A set of methods that can automatically detect patterns in data, then use the uncovered patterns to predict future data, or to perform other kinds of decision making under uncertainty.

03AI, ML and DL

Rule-based programs — AI, but not ML

  • Expert systems, e.g. medical diagnosis (if–then).

Classical ML

  • Linear regression, Logistic regression
  • Decision trees, Random forests
  • k-nearest neighbours (k-NN)
  • Support vector machines (SVM)

Deep learning

  • Computer vision — image classification, object detection
  • Natural language processing — large language models, translation
  • Speech and audio — speech recognition, generation

06Types of learning

Supervised learning

Data comes labelled: $\{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}$ for $i = 1 \dots n$. Learn the mapping $x \mapsto y$.

  • Regression — $y$ is continuous.
  • Classification — $y$ is categorical.

Unsupervised learning

No labels, only $\{x^{(i)}\}$. Learn underlying structure.

  • Clustering — group similar samples.
  • Dimensionality reduction — find a lower-dimensional representation.

07Vectors and dot products

  1. Every sample is a vector.
  2. Every dataset is a matrix.
  3. Every model relies heavily on $w^\top x + b$ — a dot product.

A vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is an ordered list of $d$ numbers.

Dot product $$ u^\top v = \sum_{i=1}^d u_i v_i = \|u\| \|v\| \cos \theta $$
  • $u^\top v = 0 \text{ if } u \perp v$
  • $x^\top x = \|x\|_2^2$
  • $\text{cosine similarity}(u, v) = \frac{u^\top v}{\|u\| \|v\|} \in [-1, 1]$
Dot product Cosine similarity
Formula $\sum_i u_i v_i$ $\frac{u^\top v}{\|u\| \|v\|}$
Range $(-\infty, +\infty)$ $[-1, +1]$
Sensitive to length Yes No
Interpretation How much do these align, weighted by size? How aligned are these, regardless of size?

08Norms

Vector subtraction

What a norm is

A norm is used to measure how big a vector is, and to measure how far one vector is from another. Formally:

$f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}, \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\forall \alpha \in \mathbb{R}$
  1. Non-negativity — $f(x) \ge 0$
  2. Definiteness — $f(x) = 0 \iff x = 0$
  3. Triangle inequality — $f(x + y) \le f(x) + f(y)$
  4. Absolute homogeneity — $f(\alpha x) = |\alpha| f(x)$
Worked example — how big is a vector? $$ \begin{aligned} v &= [3, 4]^\top \\ \|v\| &= \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5 \end{aligned} $$

Note on max norm: If we want $\|x\|_\infty = 1$, then either $|x_1|$ or $|x_2|$ (or both) must equal 1.

Worked example

$x = [2, -3, 6]^\top$ $$ \begin{aligned} \|x\|_1 &= 2 + 3 + 6 = \mathbf{11} \\ \|x\|_2 &= \sqrt{4 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{49} = \mathbf{7} \\ \|x\|_\infty &= \max(2, 3, 6) = \mathbf{6} \end{aligned} $$
The norm is non-increasing in p $$ \|x\|_1 \ge \|x\|_2 \ge \dots \ge \|x\|_\infty $$

09The Lp family

$$ \|x\|_p = \left( \sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|^p \right)^{1/p} \quad ; \quad p \ge 1 $$
L3 norm $$ \sqrt[3]{|x_1|^3 + |x_2|^3 + \dots + |x_d|^3} $$

Why the norm shrinks as p grows

$$ u = \frac{x}{\|x\|_p} \implies \|u\|_p = 1 \implies |u_i| \le 1 $$

For $0 < p < 1$, it is not a norm

Counterexample with $p = 0.5$ $$ \begin{aligned} x &= [1, 0]^\top, \quad y = [0, 1]^\top \\ \|x + y\|_{0.5} &= (\sqrt{1} + \sqrt{1})^2 = 4 \\ \|x\|_{0.5} + \|y\|_{0.5} &= 1 + 1 = 2 \end{aligned} $$
$4 > 2 \implies$ not a norm (the triangle inequality fails)

10Mean and variance

Mean — the average of a random variable

Sample Mean $$ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i $$
Population Mean $$ \mu = \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x P(X = x) $$

Variance

How much a random variable deviates from its mean.

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(X) &= \mathbb{E}[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 ] \\ \text{Var}(X) &= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 \end{aligned} $$

Standard deviation

$$ \sigma_x = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)} = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 ]} $$

Covariance

How two variables move together.

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Cov}(X, Y) &= \mathbb{E}[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y]) ] \\ \text{Cov}(X, Y) &= \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] \end{aligned} $$

Scaling

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[aX] &= a\mathbb{E}[X] \\ \text{Var}(aX) &= a^2\text{Var}(X) \end{aligned} $$
Derivation $$ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(aX) &= \mathbb{E}[ (aX - \mathbb{E}[aX])^2 ] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[ (aX - a\mathbb{E}[X])^2 ] \\ &= a^2 \mathbb{E}[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 ] \\ &= a^2 \text{Var}(X) \end{aligned} $$

Variance of a sum

$$ \text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + \text{Cov}(X, Y) + \text{Cov}(Y, X) $$
Derivation $$ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(x + y) &= \mathbb{E}[ ((x + y) - \mathbb{E}[x + y])^2 ] \\ & \quad \text{(but } \mathbb{E}[x + y] = \mathbb{E}[x] + \mathbb{E}[y] \text{)} \\ &= \mathbb{E}[ ((x + y) - \mathbb{E}[x] - \mathbb{E}[y])^2 ] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[ ((x - \mathbb{E}[x]) + (y - \mathbb{E}[y]))^2 ] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[ (x - \mathbb{E}[x])^2 + (y - \mathbb{E}[y])^2 + 2(x - \mathbb{E}[x])(y - \mathbb{E}[y]) ] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[ (x - \mathbb{E}[x])^2 ] + \mathbb{E}[ (y - \mathbb{E}[y])^2 ] + 2\mathbb{E}[ (x - \mathbb{E}[x])(y - \mathbb{E}[y]) ] \end{aligned} $$
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(X + Y) &= \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\text{Cov}(X, Y) \\ \text{Var}(X - Y) &= \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) - 2\text{Cov}(X, Y) \end{aligned} $$

11Correlation

Normalized covariance.

$$ \rho_{x,y} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_x \sigma_y} \quad ; \quad -1 \le \rho \le 1 $$
  1. $\rho = +1$ — Positive linear relationship.
  2. $\rho = -1$ — Negative linear relationship.
  3. $\rho = 0$ — No linear relationship.

Three things to remember

  1. Independence $\implies$ zero correlation.
  2. Zero correlation $\not\implies$ independence.
  3. Correlation does not imply causation.

Counterexample for (2)

$$ \begin{aligned} X &\sim \mathcal{U}\{-1, 0, 1\}, \quad Y = X^2 \\ \mathbb{E}[X] &= 0 \\ \mathbb{E}[XY] &= \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0 \\ \text{Cov}(X, Y) &= \mathbb{E}[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y]) ] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[XY] \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} $$

Insight: $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$ — knowing $X$ tells you $Y$ exactly — yet the covariance is zero. Correlation only sees straight lines.
